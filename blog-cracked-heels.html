Cracked heels: why they happen and how to fix them

4 min read  ·  By Linda Pearson

Cracked heels are one of the most common things we see, and one of the most frustrating for people at home - the creams don't seem to work, and just as they start to improve, they split again. Here's what's actually going on, and what genuinely helps.

What causes them

The skin around the heel is naturally under a lot of pressure. Every step pushes the heel pad outwards, and skin that has become dry loses its elasticity - so instead of flexing, it splits. Those splits are called fissures.

Several things make it more likely. Open-backed shoes, sandals and flip-flops let the heel spread sideways with nothing to hold it, which is why cracked heels often turn up in summer. Standing for long periods on hard floors does the same over time. Carrying extra weight increases the pressure on the heel pad. And some skin conditions, along with diabetes and reduced circulation, make dry skin more likely in the first place.

Once hard skin builds up around the edge of the heel, it makes things worse rather than better - thickened skin is less flexible, so it cracks more readily than healthy skin would.

The short version

Cracked heels are dry, thickened skin splitting under pressure. A good moisturising cream used daily is the single most effective thing you can do - and creams containing urea are designed for exactly this job. If the cracks are deep, painful or bleeding, don't wait it out.

What helps at home

Moisturise every day, and keep it up - this is the step people abandon as soon as things look better, which is exactly when the problem returns. Ordinary body lotion is usually not enough for heels. Look for a heel balm or cream containing urea, which both softens hard skin and helps the skin hold on to moisture. Products of this kind are widely available, and we stock a range at the clinic if you'd like a recommendation for your particular skin.

Apply it after a bath or shower while the skin is still slightly damp, and rub it in properly rather than leaving a layer sitting on the surface. Avoid putting cream between the toes, where the skin needs to stay dry.

Gently reducing hard skin helps the cream work, but do it carefully. A foot file or pumice used lightly on softened skin is enough. Never use scissors, razors or blades on your own feet - it is very easy to take off too much, and an open wound on the heel is a far bigger problem than a dry one.

Footwear makes a real difference too. Shoes that hold the heel in place, rather than open-backed ones, reduce the sideways spread that causes the splitting. If you love your sandals, look for a pair with a proper back or a wide, supportive heel seat.

An important exception

If you have diabetes, poor circulation, or any loss of feeling in your feet, please don't treat cracked heels yourself with files, pumice stones or over-the-counter acid preparations. Reduced sensation means damage can happen without you feeling it, and cracks can become an entry point for infection. Get them looked at professionally instead - this is precisely what regular foot checks are for.

When to see a podiatrist

It's worth booking an appointment if the cracks are deep or painful, if they're bleeding, if there's any redness, swelling or heat around them, or if daily creaming hasn't improved things after a couple of weeks.

In clinic, the hard skin around the fissures can be reduced safely and painlessly, which allows the healthy skin underneath to knit back together - something creams alone often can't achieve once the build-up is established. Just as importantly, we can work out why it happened in your case, so it's less likely to keep coming back.
